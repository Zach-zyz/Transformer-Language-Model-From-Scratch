> **TA MODEL - SYNTHETIC DATA FOR INSTRUCTION ONLY**  
> Every person, quotation, timestamp, and event below is fictional but plausible. This model demonstrates structure, specificity, separation of observation from interpretation, and appropriate synthesis. It is not evidence from real fieldwork and must not be submitted as student-collected data. Students must conduct their own observations and interview real people. ## Scope and Working Research Question

**Working research question:** How do classroom structure, social visibility, timing, and students' interpretations of risk shape whether college students ask questions during class?

**Population and context:** Undergraduate and graduate students in lecture, seminar, and recitation settings. The inquiry focuses on spoken questions addressed to an instructor or teaching assistant during the scheduled class period. Questions asked privately after class, to peers, or through digital channels are recorded as alternative help-seeking behaviors rather than treated as equivalent to public questions.

**Ethical and practical boundaries:** The observer obtained instructor permission, sat in a non-disruptive location, recorded no names or identifying details, did not photograph faces or screens, and did not infer internal states from appearance. Interpretive thoughts are labeled **[Memo]** and kept separate from directly observable actions.

## 1.3 Observation Summary

### Session O1 Summary

The first observation took place during the middle of a large introductory lecture on Monday morning. Approximately 118 students were present. During the 15-minute window, the instructor gave three broad opportunities for response or questions, generally pausing between two and four seconds. One student completed a public question. Three other students began or appeared to begin raising a hand but lowered it before being acknowledged. In contrast, at least five instances of peer consultation, backward searching through notes, or other self-help behavior were visible.

The public question produced information that appeared useful beyond the questioner: several students began writing when the instructor explained an exception not shown on the slide. The session therefore did not show an absence of uncertainty. Instead, uncertainty was frequently handled through quieter channels that did not interrupt the lecture.

### Session O2 Summary

The second observation occurred near the end of a later meeting in the same lecture hall. Two students asked questions in quick succession after the class was split between two answer choices. The first question requested repetition; the second student prefaced the question with "This might be obvious." The instructor normalized the uncertainty and added a diagram. Several students took notes during both answers, suggesting the questions were relevant to more people than the speakers.

As the scheduled end approached, opportunities narrowed. One raised hand was deferred and not revisited, and a final invitation was framed as time for "one last question." No one responded publicly, yet four students approached the instructor after dismissal. This session showed that question-asking changed across the class period and that silence during a public invitation did not mean students had no questions.

### Session O3 Summary

The third observation took place in an 18-person humanities seminar. Students asked many questions in pairs, and three questions or question-framed contributions entered the whole-group discussion. The instructor used longer wait time, invited students who had not spoken, and treated uncertainty and disagreement as useful. Even so, one student who had actively questioned a peer did not initially speak to the class, and another declined when the instructor directly invited them.

An anonymous index-card activity changed participation. The instructor read a recurring question without naming its authors, after which a previously quiet student joined the discussion. The observation suggests that small group talk, anonymous input, and public discussion enabled different kinds of participation rather than serving as interchangeable formats.

### Session O4 Summary

The fourth observation occurred in an optional technical recitation organized around table-based problem solving. Students frequently asked peers for help, but they were less likely to call the TA. One student's hand was not noticed while the TA was assisting another table, and the student switched to peer help. When the TA generalized a difficulty seen at multiple tables, students corrected their work without any individual being singled out.

An anonymous online channel received two entries shortly after the TA displayed it. The TA read one conceptual question that had four upvotes, and a student then asked a related follow-up aloud. The sequence showed how an anonymous channel could reveal shared uncertainty and create an opening for spoken discussion. It also showed a limitation: the TA had to notice, select, and integrate the digital questions into the live lesson.

## 1.4 Observation Reflection

Before observing, I framed the problem mainly as students being afraid of looking uninformed. The sessions complicated that assumption. Public visibility mattered, but question-asking also depended on timing, whether the instructor noticed a bid to speak, how long the pause lasted, whether the student could formulate a question while still processing new material, and whether another help channel was faster. I also saw evidence that silence is ambiguous. Students who never addressed the instructor still compared notes, searched prior material, wrote reminders, submitted anonymous questions, or waited after class.

Observation revealed details that an interview or literature review alone would be unlikely to reproduce accurately: a half-raised hand that disappeared, a deferred question that was never revisited, the difference between a two-second and seven-second pause, and how one question changed several other students' note-taking. These moments suggest that reluctance should not be treated only as an individual trait. Classroom pacing, spatial arrangement, turn-taking conventions, and the availability of lower-risk channels jointly shape whether uncertainty becomes visible.

My observation also had limits. I could not know why any particular student stayed silent, and I may have missed subtle help-seeking from my seat. Next time, I would use two observers with a shared coding sheet, observe full class periods rather than selected windows, and sample courses across discipline, class size, language background, and participation policy. I would also distinguish students who prefer private help from students who want to speak but cannot find an opening. Any design direction should support multiple forms of participation without treating public speaking as the only legitimate behavior or forcing students to disclose confusion.

# 2. Interviews / Contextual Inquiry

## 2.1 Interview Protocol

**Opening script:** Thank you for speaking with me. I am studying how college students decide whether to ask questions during class. I am interested in your experiences, not in evaluating you or your instructors. You may skip any question or stop at any time. I will remove identifying details. May I audio-record this conversation so I can review my notes? The recording will only be used for this class research activity.

**Core questions**

1. Think about your most recent class. Can you walk me through any moments when something was unclear?
2. Tell me about the last time you asked a question aloud during class. What happened before, during, and after?
3. Tell me about a recent time you had a question but did not ask it during class. What did you do instead?
4. How do you decide whether a question is worth asking publicly?
5. What signals from an instructor make it easier or harder for you to ask?
6. How do the size, layout, pace, and format of a class affect what you do?
7. How do other students influence whether you ask a question?
8. What role, if any, do office hours, discussion boards, group chats, search tools, or anonymous-question systems play?
9. Have language, prior preparation, identity, accessibility needs, or past classroom experiences shaped how you participate? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.
10. What do you value most when you need help during class?
11. If you could change the way questions work in one of your classes, what would you change, and why?
12. Is there anything important about asking questions in class that I did not ask?

**Neutral follow-ups used as appropriate:** "Can you give me a specific example?" "What happened next?" "What were you thinking at that moment?" "How did you respond?" "Why was that important to you?" "Was that typical or unusual?"

### Participant and setting

- **Approximate age:** 20

### Marked-up interview guide

**Q1. Think about your most recent class. Can you walk me through any moments when something was unclear?**

- **Notes:** Statistics lecture, approximately 150 students. Lost track when instructor moved from formula to software output. Wrote "why?" in notes. Continued copying because next slide appeared.
- **Follow-up:** What happened to the question later?
- **Notes:** Looked at textbook that evening; still partly unsure; planned to ask friend before homework.
- **Important quote:** "If I stop to figure out the question, I miss the next thing too."

**Q2. Tell me about the last time you asked a question aloud during class. What happened before, during, and after?**

- **Notes:** About three weeks earlier in a 24-person class. Instructor had already heard two questions. P01 asked for an example. Instructor responded positively; another student added a follow-up. P01 felt relieved afterward.
- **Follow-up:** Why ask in that class but not statistics?
- **Notes:** Knew classmates; instructor used names; question felt connected to discussion rather than an interruption.
- **Important quote:** "It already felt like a conversation, so I wasn't changing the mood by speaking."

**Q3. Tell me about a recent time you had a question but did not ask it during class. What did you do instead?**

- **Notes:** In statistics lecture, wondered why an answer choice was wrong. Did not ask because instructor had said they were behind schedule. Texted class group chat; two others had same question. One shared a video later.
- **Follow-up:** Did the alternative fully answer it?
- **Notes:** Helped complete homework but P01 was unsure about underlying concept.
- **Important quote:** "I found enough to move on, not enough to feel like I understood it."

**Q4. How do you decide whether a question is worth asking publicly?**

- **Notes:** Checks whether answer is on slide; whether question is personal confusion or broadly useful; whether instructor seems rushed; whether P01 can phrase it in one sentence. Hesitates if question requires explaining a long chain of misunderstanding.
- **Follow-up:** How do you know if others would benefit?
- **Notes:** Looks for people flipping pages or whispering; sometimes waits for someone else to ask.
- **Important quote:** "I feel like I need evidence that it isn't just me."

**Q5. What signals from an instructor make it easier or harder for you to ask?**

- **Notes:** Easier: instructor pauses, looks around, says where confusion is common, thanks questioner, returns to deferred questions. Harder: asks "Any questions?" while changing slides, says material is easy, answers own question quickly, jokes about poor exam performance.
- **Follow-up:** Which signal has strongest effect?
- **Notes:** Seeing instructor handle another student's question respectfully.
- **Important quote:** "The first question tells me what kind of room it is."

**Q6. How do the size, layout, pace, and format of a class affect what you do?**

- **Notes:** In large hall feels voice must be confident and concise. Cannot see instructor's reaction clearly from rear. Fast pace makes question expire. Small class easier if seated in circle, but also harder to hide after speaking.
- **Follow-up:** Is small always better?
- **Notes:** No. In required participation seminars, feels pressured to perform rather than genuinely ask.

**Q7. How do other students influence whether you ask a question?**

- **Notes:** Friends nearby can confirm question. If classmates sigh, pack up, or appear to understand, P01 stays quiet. Worries about holding people after class. Questions from high-performing students make question-asking seem legitimate but can also raise standard for what counts as a "good" question.
- **Important quote:** "I'm not only asking the professor; I'm asking in front of everyone who wants to leave."

**Q8. What role do office hours, discussion boards, group chats, search tools, or anonymous-question systems play?**

- **Notes:** Group chat is fastest and lowest risk. Search is useful for definitions. Office hours feel high effort and conflict with work shift twice per week. Discussion board seems permanent and attached to name. Has used anonymous form once and liked seeing upvotes.
- **Follow-up:** What was missing from the anonymous form?
- **Notes:** Instructor answered only selected questions; no indication when unanswered ones would be addressed.

**Q9. Have prior preparation or past classroom experiences shaped how you participate?**

- **Notes:** A high-school teacher once responded, "We covered that yesterday." Since then, P01 rehearses questions mentally. In courses outside major, assumes classmates have more background.
- **Important quote:** "One bad answer can follow you into classes with completely different professors."

**Q10. What do you value most when you need help during class?**

- **Notes:** Quick confirmation of where reasoning went wrong; not being singled out; ability to continue following lecture; answer accessible later.

**Q11. If you could change the way questions work in one class, what would you change, and why?**

- **Notes:** Add two planned question pauses after examples; allow anonymous submission; instructor should mark answered/unanswered and revisit top unanswered questions. Does not want forced cold-calling.

**Q12. Is there anything important that I did not ask?**

- **Notes:** Sometimes chooses not to ask because learning privately is preferred, not because of fear. A system should not make silent students feel defective.
- **Important quote:** "Quiet doesn't always mean blocked. Sometimes I just want time."

## 2.3 Interview 1 Summary

P01 was a 20-year-old second-year undergraduate and an acquaintance of the interviewer. The 36-minute interview took place in a quiet student-center study room and was recorded with permission. P01 regularly attends both large lectures and small discussion courses. They described question-asking as a rapid judgment involving content, timing, audience, and the instructor's behavior rather than a stable willingness or unwillingness to speak.

Three findings were especially important. First, processing time and lecture pace can prevent a student from forming a usable question before the class moves on: "If I stop to figure out the question, I miss the next thing too." Second, P01 looked for social proof that confusion was shared and used classmates' behavior to judge whether a question was legitimate. Third, alternative channels reduced immediate social risk but did not always support deep understanding. Group chat helped P01 finish work, while office hours conflicted with employment and a named discussion board felt too permanent. P01 wanted predictable pauses and a transparent anonymous channel, but rejected the assumption that every quiet student should be pushed to speak.

- **Follow-up:** Why prepare it in writing?
- **Notes:** Helps make wording concise and reduces worry about pronunciation or forgetting key term.

**Q3. Tell me about a recent time you had a question but did not ask it. What did you do instead?**

- **Notes:** Did not understand an idiom in instructor's explanation. Unsure if it mattered technically. Searched phrase later; did not ask anyone. In another case, wrote question and asked TA after class.
- **Important quote:** "First I must decide whether the problem is the concept or only my English."

**Q4. How do you decide whether a question is worth asking publicly?**

- **Notes:** Asks if question affects assignment or central concept; if can state it clearly; if class has time; if instructor welcomes foundational questions. Avoids asking about terminology when peers seem comfortable.
- **Follow-up:** What makes a question feel "foundational"?
- **Notes:** Concern that classmates learned it in undergraduate course; fear of revealing background gap.

**Q5. What signals from an instructor make it easier or harder for you to ask?**

- **Notes:** Easier when instructor previews a pause, gives one minute to write, repeats question before answering, and admits a concept is difficult. Harder when instructor speaks over beginning of question, corrects wording before addressing meaning, or says "obviously."
- **Important quote:** "When the professor repeats my question kindly, I know the class heard the idea and not only my accent."

**Q6. How do size, layout, pace, and format affect what you do?**

- **Notes:** Online chat was easiest because could edit before sending. Large hall difficult acoustically; does not know how loudly to speak. Seminar easier after trust develops, harder in first weeks. Pair discussion helps test vocabulary.
- **Follow-up:** Does technology solve the problem?
- **Notes:** Not by itself. Chat can be ignored; real-name tools preserve exposure; simultaneous lecture and chat creates cognitive load.

**Q7. How do other students influence whether you ask?**

- **Notes:** Hearing another international student ask helps. Fast answers from peers can make P02 feel behind. A friend sitting nearby can review wording. If others react impatiently, waits until after class.

**Q8. What role do office hours and other help channels play?**

- **Notes:** Office hours valuable for complex questions but intimidating if no prepared agenda. TA forum helpful because answers remain available. Private message feels safest but may duplicate questions. Anonymous voting useful for shared issues.
- **Follow-up:** What would make office hours more accessible?
- **Notes:** Example topics, appointment slots, permission to bring incomplete questions, remote option.

**Q9. Have language, identity, accessibility needs, or prior experiences shaped participation?**

- **Notes:** Additional planning needed in English. Does not want instructors to reduce intellectual level of answer. Has experienced classmate finishing sentence; intended as help but felt loss of control. Cultural expectation from prior education was to ask after class rather than interrupt.
- **Important quote:** "I want more time, not a simpler answer."

**Q10. What do you value most when you need help during class?**

- **Notes:** Time to formulate; accurate understanding; respectful response; option to choose public or private route; assurance unanswered question is not lost.

**Q11. If you could change the way questions work, what would you change?**

- **Notes:** Instructor gives prompt, then 60 seconds for everyone to write; students can submit or speak. Instructor groups similar questions and says when remaining questions will be answered. Pair rehearsal before whole-class discussion.

**Q12. Is there anything important that I did not ask?**

- **Notes:** Asking a question can be contribution, not confession. In a well-facilitated seminar, prepared question helped peers. Would like instructors to frame questions as knowledge-building.
- **Important quote:** "A question can show work, but many classrooms make it feel like showing a hole."

## 2.5 Interview 2 Summary

P02 was a 25-year-old first-year master's student recruited through a campus study notice. The 41-minute interview was conducted in a campus library room and recorded with permission. P02 is an international student who uses English as an additional language and participates in both technical lectures and discussion seminars. P02 emphasized that reluctance was often a timing mismatch: understanding enough to formulate a question took longer than the pause provided.

Three findings stood out. First, question opportunities often occurred before P02 had converted uncertainty into concise English: "My question arrives later than the question period." Second, language influenced preparation and social exposure but did not imply a need for simplified content; P02 stated, "I want more time, not a simpler answer." Third, channel design mattered only when paired with facilitation. Editable chat, anonymous voting, written reflection, and pair rehearsal could help, but only if instructors monitored them, grouped related questions, and explained when unanswered questions would be addressed. P02 also reframed questions as contributions to collective learning rather than admissions of deficiency.

## 2.6 Interview Reflection

The interviews shifted my design judgment from "make students less afraid" to "support students in recognizing, forming, and routing questions under different constraints." P01 described needing social evidence that a question was shared, while P02 described a delay between confusion and the ability to phrase a question. Both accounts help explain behaviors seen in observation, such as peer checking, half-raised hands, notes saved for later, and activity after class. Interviews provided motives and histories that observation could not establish, including employment conflicts with office hours, prior dismissive responses, language-planning effort, and a cultural norm of asking after class.

I was surprised that several common interventions could create new problems. A named discussion board may feel more permanent than a spoken question; cold-calling may increase participation counts while reducing autonomy; a live chat may compete with attention; and anonymous tools can become a dead end if unanswered items disappear. The participants also challenged the assumption that public speech is always the desired outcome. Both valued having a choice among public, anonymous, peer, and private routes. A humane design should make uncertainty addressable without requiring one "ideal" participation style.

Next time, I would ask participants to reconstruct one recent class using a timeline and identify the exact moment a question formed, changed, or expired. I would also recruit students with a wider range of disabilities, course loads, commuting responsibilities, race and gender identities, and prior educational cultures, rather than treating two accounts as representative. During these interviews I occasionally moved too quickly from a negative experience to a proposed feature. In a future study, I would stay longer with the participant's workaround, what it protects, and what trade-offs it creates before asking for design ideas.

# 3. Cross-Method Takeaways for Teaching Discussion

These are **provisional interpretations**, not final claims. A full D1 report would triangulate them with the other teammates' fieldwork and the secondary review.

1. **Silence is not the absence of questions.** Students may use peer talk, notes, search, anonymous systems, or after-class queues while remaining publicly silent.
2. **A question has a time window.** Students need time to notice confusion, decide whether it matters, formulate language, and enter the conversation before the topic moves on.
3. **Social risk is produced by the setting.** Visibility, pace, classmates' reactions, instructor language, and whether earlier questions were respected all affect the perceived cost of speaking.
4. **Alternative channels need facilitation.** Anonymous or digital tools help only when students know the question was received, understand when it will be answered, and can follow up.
5. **Equity is not served by forcing one participation mode.** Students differ in language-processing time, disability access needs, prior preparation, employment schedules, cultural norms, and preference for reflection. A good classroom should offer meaningful choices without lowering intellectual expectations.

## Candidate User Requirements Derived from This Example

- Students need a way to capture a question at the moment it forms and return to it after the lecture has advanced.
- Students need multiple low-friction routes - spoken, written, anonymous, peer, and private - with clear expectations about who will respond and when.
- Students need instructors to normalize uncertainty through sufficient wait time, respectful responses, and visible follow-through on deferred questions.
- Instructors need a manageable way to identify shared confusion without monitoring several competing channels or exposing individual students unnecessarily.

## What Makes This a Strong Model

- Raw observation is timestamped and keeps behavior separate from inference.
- Summaries describe what happened before explaining what it might mean.
- Interview questions are open-ended, non-leading, and grounded in recent experiences.
- Each interview includes context, duration, notes, follow-ups, quotations, and at least three findings without personally identifiable information.
- Reflections explain how the method changed the researcher's understanding and acknowledge limitations, equity, dignity, and next-step improvements.
- The evidence does not jump directly to a single product idea. It preserves a design space for later synthesis.
